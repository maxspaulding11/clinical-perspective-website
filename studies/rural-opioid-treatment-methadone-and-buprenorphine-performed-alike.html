In rural opioid treatment, methadone and buprenorphine performed alike, while the clinic predicted clean urine tests

Covered November 10, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Records from 561 patients at four rural Oregon clinics showed no difference in retention or opioid use between methadone and buprenorphine/naloxone over a year. Clinic site predicted the proportion of negative urine tests.

Methadone and buprenorphine are the two most effective medications for opioid use disorder, but little is known about how they compare in community-based rural treatment settings. This retrospective cohort study examined outcomes for 561 individuals who began treatment between January and December 2019 at four medication clinics in rural Oregon, using data extracted from electronic medical records and covering the first year of treatment. Of the 561 patients, 74.5% were dispensed methadone and 25.5% were prescribed buprenorphine/naloxone. The groups differed at intake in two ways: those on methadone were more likely to be unemployed, 57.7% against 44.8%, and more likely to have a diagnosis of severe opioid use disorder, 93.5% against 83.9%. There were no other significant differences in intake demographics. Over the following 12 months, the two medications produced no significant group differences in opioid use or treatment retention. Clinic site, however, was a significant predictor of the proportion of negative urine tests. The authors conclude that both medications performed comparably well in reducing opioid use and keeping people in treatment, and that clinical site and time in treatment had a larger impact on outcomes than medication type. The design limits the strength of that conclusion. Patients were not randomised, so the groups were not comparable at the start, and the higher severity in the methadone group could mask a difference in either direction. The data come from one state and from a single year of admissions. Even so, the suggestion that the clinic may matter more than the molecule is an important one for planning rural services, and it suggests that expanding access to both medications, and supporting the clinics that deliver them, are the more useful levers.
