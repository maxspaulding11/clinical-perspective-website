Treatment

Re-run against the same criteria, no couple therapy model met the bar for strong empirical support

Covered November 20, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Replicating a 1998 review with 40 studies and the same APA criteria, researchers found no couple therapy model met the standard for strong empirical support. Seven, including emotionally focused and Gottman, had modest support.

In 1998, Baucom and colleagues evaluated the evidence for couple therapies for relationship distress using the Chambless criteria, the primary framework the American Psychological Association has used to classify empirically supported treatments. That review found some models met the standard for strong support. This systematic review replicated and updated the evaluation, applying the same criteria to 40 eligible studies, and reached a different conclusion: no couple therapy model met the standards for strong empirical support. Seven models demonstrated modest support: behavioural, cognitive-behavioural, emotionally focused, Gottman, integrative behavioural, insight-oriented and strategic couple therapies. The most frequent limitations were insufficient sample size and lack of independent replication. It would be easy to over-read this result. The review does not show that couple therapy is ineffective. Modest support means there is evidence of benefit, just not enough of the kind these criteria require to reach the higher tier. The authors also point to limitations of the Chambless criteria themselves, and recommend evaluating couple therapies with the recently adopted Tolin criteria, which synthesise a broader base of evidence through meta-analysis and place more emphasis on clinical significance. That newer approach may give a fuller picture of how much these therapies help. Still, the finding is a useful check. Several of these models are familiar by name, and a familiar name is not the same as strong support. More than two decades after an earlier review rated some approaches as strongly supported, none currently reaches that tier under the same rules. The authors call for rigorous, large-scale studies with more diverse couples, which would strengthen the evidence base and make it more likely that results generalise to the couples who actually seek help. Until then, the honest description of the evidence for each of these models is modest, not strong.

Jeffrey B Jackson, et al., Journal of marital and family therapy, October 2026
