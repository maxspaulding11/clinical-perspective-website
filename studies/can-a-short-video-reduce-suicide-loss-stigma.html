Stigma

Can a short video reduce suicide-loss stigma?

Covered August 13, 2026

A large randomized trial tested whether brief video content could shift the judgment suicide-loss survivors face.

People who lose someone to suicide carry a heavy grief, and often a second burden: public stigma, the subtle judgment that they were somehow disconnected, weak, or to blame. This large randomized trial asked whether something as simple as a short video could soften that stigma. Researchers recruited 1,351 adults aged 18–50 and randomly showed them either a brief first-person video of a suicide-loss survivor sharing their story, or a psychoeducational control video. Compared with the control, people who watched the survivor’s narrative showed clear reductions in stigma right afterward and still partially at 30 days. The biggest shifts were in specific judgments: seeing survivors as “disconnected” fell by about 24% and “cowardly” by 16%, with smaller but real drops in viewing them as “immoral” or “irresponsible.” The effect faded somewhat over the month, suggesting these messages need repeating to stick. Still, the promise is significant: a low-cost, easily shared video, the kind of thing that fits naturally on social media, can measurably move a community from blame toward empathy. For survivors, that shift in the social climate around them isn’t just kind; it can support their recovery.

The study

Yossi Levi-Belz, et al., Depression and Anxiety, July 2026

DOI: 10.1155/da/2062450
