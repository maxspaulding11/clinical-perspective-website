Brain chemistry differences across anxiety disorders

Covered August 1, 2026

25 brain-imaging studies pooled to compare choline and other chemical levels across social, generalized, and panic anxiety.

Researchers pooled data from 25 brain-imaging studies (using a technique called proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy, which measures brain chemical levels non-invasively) covering social anxiety disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and panic disorder, comparing 370 people with these conditions to 342 healthy controls. The standout finding: choline-containing compounds, molecules involved in maintaining cell membranes and supporting brain signaling, were consistently lower across cortical brain regions in people with any of the three anxiety disorders, especially in the prefrontal cortex, with about an 8% average reduction. Notably, this pattern held steady across all three diagnoses, suggesting it's a shared feature of anxiety in general rather than specific to one disorder. The researchers propose that chronic anxiety keeps the brain's arousal system on overdrive, and stress-related brain chemicals like norepinephrine may increase the brain's demand for choline faster than it can be replenished, gradually depleting it. They also found a related dip in a marker of neuronal health. While this doesn't prove cause and effect, the authors suggest it raises an interesting future question: could choline supplementation eventually have a role in treating anxiety, an idea worth further dedicated research before anyone draws conclusions.

The study

Molecular Psychiatry, Vol. 30, pages 6020-6032 (Issue 12), December 2025

DOI: 10.1038/s41380-025-03206-7
