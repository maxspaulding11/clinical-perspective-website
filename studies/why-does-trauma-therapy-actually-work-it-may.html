Why does trauma therapy actually work? It may come down to changing beliefs

Covered August 28, 2026

A meta-analysis pooling 44 studies and over 5,000 participants found that the more a person's trauma-related beliefs shifted during therapy, the more their PTSD symptoms improved (r = .45).

Trauma-focused therapies like prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy clearly help many people with PTSD, but researchers have long debated exactly why they work. One leading theory is that these therapies succeed by changing distorted trauma-related beliefs, thoughts like "the world is completely dangerous" or "it was my fault." This study pooled data from 44 existing trials and studies, covering 95 separate effect-size estimates and more than 5,000 participants, to test that theory directly using a three-level meta-analysis (a statistical method built for combining results that aren't fully independent, like multiple measurements from the same study). The finding: the amount a person's trauma-related cognitions shifted during treatment was moderately and reliably linked to how much their PTSD symptoms improved (r = .45), and this held up across different sensitivity checks. That doesn't prove changing beliefs causes symptom improvement rather than the reverse, since most of the underlying studies measured both at the same time points rather than tracking which shifted first. Still, it strengthens the case that helping people revise how they think about their trauma is a real engine of recovery, not just a side effect of feeling better.
