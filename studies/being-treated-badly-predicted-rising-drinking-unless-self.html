Being treated badly predicted rising drinking -- unless self-control was quick

Covered September 29, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Following 133 college students for three years, those reporting more social mistreatment showed steeper increases in alcohol problems. Faster response inhibition on a lab task blunted that link -- but the crucial three-way test was not significant (p = 0.10).

Being belittled, excluded or treated unfairly is a reliable predictor of heavy drinking, but not everyone responds that way, and researchers led by Chia-Chuan Yu asked whether the difference lies in self-regulation. They followed 133 college students for three years, measuring alcohol problems annually with the AUDIT and social mistreatment alongside it, and at baseline put everyone through a Stop-Signal Task in an MRI scanner. That task yields two measures: stop-signal reaction time, essentially how fast someone can cancel an action already underway, and post-error slowing, how much they pause after getting one wrong. Drinking problems rose across the three years, and the rise was steeper for students reporting more mistreatment, a significant interaction adding about 2.67% of explained variance. Inhibition mattered too: students with faster stop-signal times showed flatter drinking trajectories, and there was a significant interaction between inhibition and mistreatment, suggesting quicker self-control blunted the effect of being treated badly. The interpretation needs restraint on exactly the point that makes the finding interesting. The three-way test -- whether inhibition changes how mistreatment shapes drinking over time -- came out non-significant at p = 0.10. So the moderation is visible in the cross-section but not confirmed in the trajectory, and the authors describe the brain-behaviour analyses as exploratory throughout. They also note their p-threshold was an uncorrected 0.05 across a set of candidate moderators, which inflates the chance of a false positive. Other limits are worth carrying into any reading of it: these were relatively healthy college students, not people with diagnosed alcohol use disorder; the sample was 64.7% female and the imbalance widened at follow-up; and the mistreatment measure does not distinguish its source, so sexism, racism and ordinary interpersonal unpleasantness are pooled into one score despite plausibly acting differently.
