Feeling close to influencers was linked to both better wellbeing and more problematic use

Covered November 18, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

A meta-analysis of seventeen studies found parasocial engagement with social media influencers was associated with both positive outcomes (r = 0.38) and negative ones (r = 0.24), with the positive links stronger.

Social media influencers are part of the daily lives of billions of users, particularly adolescents and young adults, and audiences often form parasocial bonds with them, in the form of parasocial relationships and parasocial interactions. Concern about the mental health effects of these bonds is common, but no previous meta-analysis had quantified the evidence. This systematic review searched seven databases and included studies that quantitatively assessed parasocial relationships or parasocial interactions with social media influencers alongside mental health outcomes. A three-level random-effects meta-analysis pooled correlations separately for positive or adaptive outcomes and negative or maladaptive ones, and tested moderators including outcome domain, type of parasocial construct, age group, gender, cultural region and platform. Seventeen studies contributing 52 effect sizes were included. Parasocial engagement was positively associated with both positive outcomes (r = 0.38) and negative outcomes (r = 0.24), and the positive effects were significantly stronger. Wellbeing showed the largest association (r = 0.42), followed by social media addiction (r = 0.33). Parasocial interaction showed stronger associations than parasocial relationships (r = 0.56 against 0.22). Effects were largest among adolescents (r = 0.44) and in Eastern samples compared with others (r = 0.61 against 0.29). There was no evidence of publication bias. The authors conclude that parasocial engagement works as a psychological double-edged sword, reliably linked to both enhanced wellbeing and problematic engagement, and that its meaning depends on the outcome, the construct, the developmental stage and the cultural context. The main caution is about direction. The pooled studies report associations, and the authors call for longitudinal, mechanism-based research. It remains possible that people who are already doing well, or already struggling, engage with influencers differently, rather than the engagement causing either outcome.
