How early-life stress rewires the gut

Covered July 31, 2026

A mouse model of maternal separation traced the actual mechanism linking early stress to long-term digestive problems.

Researchers wanted to understand exactly how early-life stress leads to long-term digestive problems, since the gut-brain connection was known but the underlying mechanism wasn't clear. Using a mouse model of early maternal separation, they found that early stress caused heightened gut pain sensitivity, sex-specific problems with intestinal movement, and lasting structural changes to the gut's own nervous system, including more serotonin-related nerve activity. They traced this to two separate culprits: sex hormones appeared to drive the pain sensitivity (blocking gonadal hormones reversed it), while overactive sympathetic nervous system signaling drove the motility problems (blocking that signaling restored normal gut movement). Serotonin pathways seemed to influence both issues. To confirm this matters in humans, not just mice, they analyzed two large pediatric population groups and found that mothers' mental health struggles were consistently linked to higher rates of gut-brain disorders in their children, mirroring the lab findings. The bigger picture: childhood stress doesn't just affect mental health, it can physically rewire two separate nerve pathways in the gut, and because pain and motility are driven by different mechanisms, future treatments may need to target them separately rather than with one blanket approach.

The study

Najjar S, Kildegaard H, Talati A, and colleagues, Gastroenterology, March 2026

DOI: 10.1053/j.gastro.2026.02.030
