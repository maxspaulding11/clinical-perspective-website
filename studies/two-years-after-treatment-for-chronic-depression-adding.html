Two years after treatment for chronic depression, adding psychotherapy made no difference to recurrence

Covered November 12, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

In 323 people with persistent depressive disorder followed for two years, 62.8% met criteria for a major depressive episode during follow-up. Rates were similar whether they had received CBASP, supportive therapy or medication alone.

Persistent depressive disorder includes chronic major depressive episodes, recurrent episodes without full recovery between them, and so-called double depression. It is difficult to treat, and treatment guidelines disagree about the value of the cognitive behavioral analysis system of psychotherapy, or CBASP, which was developed specifically for chronic depression. The REVAMP trial gave all participants 12 weeks of open-label antidepressant medication. Those who did not remit stayed on medication and were randomised to 12 weeks of CBASP, brief supportive psychotherapy, or medication alone. The acute phase found no difference between groups. This study asked whether CBASP nonetheless protected against recurrence over the longer term. Participants who completed the randomised phase were offered two years of naturalistic follow-up, with blinded raters assessing remission status every three months using standardised interviews and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. Of 323 participants who entered follow-up, 203 (62.8%) met criteria for a current major depressive episode at some point. The rates were similar across the groups: 60.2% after CBASP, 65.3% after supportive therapy and 63.6% after medication alone, a difference that was not significant (p = 0.69). The factors that did predict lower recurrence risk were remission after acute treatment, absence of a comorbid anxiety disorder, and female gender. The authors conclude there was no long-term benefit of 12 weeks of CBASP with medication over the other treatments. Two features limit the conclusion: treatment during follow-up was naturalistic rather than controlled, and only those who completed the randomised phase were followed, so the sample is not everyone who started. Still, the long-term result echoes the short-term one. For this hard-to-treat group, the type of add-on therapy did not change the course, and achieving remission early was the strongest signal of staying well.
