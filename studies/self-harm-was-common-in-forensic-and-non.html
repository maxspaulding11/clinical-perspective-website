Forensic

Self-harm was common in forensic and non-forensic patients with schizophrenia, but was linked to different correlates in each group

Covered November 15, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Among 392 patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders in five European countries, lifetime self-harm was high in forensic and non-forensic patients alike. Personality disorder led in forensic patients; victimisation led in others.

Deliberate self-harm is a major concern among people with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, and rates are known to be high among forensic patients. Yet forensic patients are under-represented in suicide research, and it is not clear whether their risk factors differ from those of other patients. This study drew on the multinational EU-VIORMED study, conducted across five European countries, and included 392 participants aged 18 to 65 with a DSM-5 diagnosis of a schizophrenia spectrum disorder, all of whom completed standardised sociodemographic, clinical, neuropsychological and criminological assessments. Lifetime deliberate self-harm was recorded as present or absent. The researchers used separate LASSO logistic regression models, a method that selects the strongest correlates from many candidates, in the forensic and non-forensic subsamples. Lifetime self-harm was common in both: 45.9% of forensic and 39.5% of non-forensic patients. The correlates differed. In forensic patients, the strongest was comorbid personality disorder, followed by functional impairment. In non-forensic patients, interpersonal victimisation was the strongest, followed by personality disorder, male gender, lifetime substance use and some psychopathology items. Overall, self-harm among forensic patients was more closely associated with personality pathology and functioning, while among non-forensic patients it was linked to trauma, clinical severity and sociodemographic factors. The authors argue that suicide prevention should use tailored formulations: emphasising personality pathology and functioning in forensic settings, and trauma-informed, integrated care in general services. Several limitations apply. Self-harm was a lifetime yes-or-no measure, the design was cross-sectional, the two subsamples were modelled separately and their differences were not formally tested, and correlates identified by LASSO describe associations in this sample rather than validated predictors of future self-harm. Still, the study suggests that similar rates do not imply identical mechanisms, and it brings an often-excluded group into the evidence base.
