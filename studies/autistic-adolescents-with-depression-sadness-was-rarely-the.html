In autistic adolescents with depression, sadness was rarely the symptom that showed

Covered November 23, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Among 96 autistic adolescents in Hong Kong, 12.5% met criteria for major depression, and only 58.3% of them were in treatment. Their symptoms were mainly biological and cognitive; sadness was reported by only two.

Depression in autistic adolescents may not present in the familiar way, which makes it easy to miss. This study set out to estimate the prevalence and correlates of depressive disorders among adolescents with autism spectrum disorder in the Hong Kong West Cluster. Chinese adolescents aged 13 to 17 with a clinical autism diagnosis who had been seen in the preceding 18 months were invited, with their parents. Depressive disorders were diagnosed using the parent-reported Diagnostic Interview Schedule for Children, symptom severity with the self-report Beck Depression Inventory-II, and parental depression and anxiety with standard measures. Of 140 eligible adolescents, 96, 82 boys and 14 girls with a mean age of 15.7, completed the assessments. Twelve, or 12.5%, met criteria for major depressive disorder, and only 58.3% of them were receiving medication or psychological treatment. The most commonly reported depressive symptoms were biological and cognitive, while sadness was reported by only two participants. Predictors of depression were maternal education at bachelor's level or above, a paternal history of mental disorder, and higher maternal depressive scores, with female sex and paternal anxiety showing non-significant trends. The authors advise clinicians not to rely solely on self-reported sadness when assessing autistic adolescents, and suggest that parental mental health may be both a clinical priority and a route to prevention. The study's size constrains what it can show. With only 12 adolescents meeting criteria, estimates of predictors are unstable; the odds ratio of 36.73 for maternal education, for instance, signals imprecision more than a precise effect. Girls made up a small share of the sample. The practical message holds. In autistic adolescents, depression may show itself through biological and cognitive symptoms rather than through sadness, and assessments should look for them directly.
