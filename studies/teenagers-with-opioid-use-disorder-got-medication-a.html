Teenagers with opioid use disorder got medication a fraction as often as adults

Covered October 7, 2026

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Of 229,847 Medicaid-enrolled young people with opioid use disorder, just 3.1% stayed on medication for six months. Among under-18s who reached treatment, 6.6% received any, against 51.2% of those over 18.

One in a hundred US adolescents and young adults has an opioid use disorder, and there are medications that reliably reduce the risk of dying from it. Researchers led by Scott Hadland used Medicaid claims from every US state between 2016 and 2023 to follow 229,847 people aged 13 to 25 with a diagnosis of opioid use disorder through what addiction medicine calls the cascade of care. Half, 116,458 or 50.7%, started treatment within fourteen days of diagnosis. A third, 75,614, engaged with it, meaning two or more further appointments inside the next month. Of those who engaged, 46.3% received medication, which is 15.2% of everyone diagnosed. The age split is the finding. Among under-18s who had engaged in treatment, 554 of 8,352 received medication: 6.6%. Among those 18 and over, 34,457 of 67,262 did: 51.2%. A teenager who reached treatment was roughly eight times less likely to be offered the medication than a young adult sitting in the same system. Buprenorphine accounted for most prescriptions, methadone a quarter. Overall just 7,070 people, 3.1% or about one in 32, stayed on medication for 180 days or more, the national quality benchmark. Retention was best on methadone at 26.2%. The youngest adolescents and racially minoritised young people progressed least far at every stage. These are administrative claims, so engagement and treatment are billing categories rather than clinical judgements, and Medicaid is not the whole population. But the measures used are the national quality measures, which means this is the system being graded against its own standard.
