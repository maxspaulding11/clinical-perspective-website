Does vitamin D help depression?

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Updated September 11, 2026 · kept current as we cover new studies

The short answer

Short answer: not as a treatment for depression on its own. In the randomized trial we have covered, eight weeks of high-dose vitamin D3 did not beat placebo for depression in young people selected for having both major depression and low vitamin D.

Two qualifications, both from that same trial. It did modestly reduce anxiety. And in the subgroup with moderate-to-severe depression, it reduced both depression and anxiety compared with placebo, although that was an exploratory subgroup analysis and the adolescent slice of it did not clearly benefit. No safety problems, such as raised calcium, appeared.

So it is a lead worth testing in larger trials for people who are genuinely deficient and more severely depressed, not a standalone treatment, and not a reason to defer established care.

What this page covers. One randomized trial in 108 people aged 10 to 24 who all had low vitamin D. It says nothing about people whose vitamin D is normal, and nothing about doses or durations other than the one tested. Every claim above is drawn from the studies below, and each of those links to the original paper.

What has been shown to help outside a therapist's office

The reason people ask about supplements is usually access. These trials test the other low-barrier options against real comparison groups.

  • Lifestyle medicine for depression, without a therapist

    Journal of Affective Disorders · July 2026

    A three-arm trial of an 8-week program delivered by app, by booklet, or not at all.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1016/j.jad.2026.122319 ↗

  • Does a mental health app still work without a coach checking in?

    PLOS Medicine · August 2026

    In a three-arm trial recruiting across 13 countries, 825 adults living with chronic medical conditions used a digital wellbeing program either on their own or with weekly phone check-ins. Both versions cut combined anxiety and depression scores by roughly 3 points versus a waitlist, and the two versions were statistically indistinguishable from each other.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1371/journal.pmed.1005198 ↗

  • An app for depression after breast cancer

    JAMA Network Open · July 2026

    313 women were enrolled in a trial of a fully automated, publicly available program — because there aren't enough therapists.

Why depression scores are slipperier than they look

Every result above is a change in a depression score, and those scores move for reasons that have nothing to do with treatment.

  • Changing the wording changes your depression score

    JAMA Network Open · July 2026

    A randomized trial altered only the instructions on the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 — and the same people scored differently.

Common questions

Does vitamin D treat depression?

Not on the evidence here. In the trial we covered it did not beat placebo for depression overall, even in a group chosen for being deficient.

Did it do anything at all?

It modestly reduced anxiety in the randomized vitamin D group, and in the moderate-to-severe depression subgroup it reduced both depression and anxiety versus placebo. That subgroup finding was exploratory, which means it is a hypothesis for the next trial rather than a result to act on.

How much was used, and was it safe?

4,800 IU of vitamin D3 daily for eight weeks. No safety problems such as hypercalcaemia appeared in this trial.

Should I have my vitamin D tested?

That is a question for a clinician, and this page cannot answer it. What the trial can tell you is that its participants were selected for having low vitamin D, so its results do not extend to people whose levels are normal.
