Is EMDR evidence-based?

By Maxamus Spaulding, BA

Updated September 9, 2026 · kept current as we cover new studies

The short answer

Short answer: yes, with a qualification that gets lost in most arguments about it. In the randomized trials that have been run, EMDR reduces PTSD symptom severity, and it clearly outperforms waiting lists and no treatment. What it has not been shown to do is outperform the other established trauma therapies it is usually compared against. Against those, it comes out roughly level.

That one fact explains most of the contradictory pages you will find on this question. A page calling EMDR highly effective is usually reporting the comparison against no treatment. A page calling it no better than ordinary therapy is usually reporting the comparison against active treatment. Both numbers come out of the same studies. The comparison chosen decides the story.

One more caveat worth carrying: the authors of the pooled analysis note that most of the trials they included had methodological weaknesses, so confidence in the size of the effect is not absolute.

What this page covers. This page covers what we have summarised. It does not review clinical practice guidelines, and it does not cover studies that try to isolate the eye movements from the rest of the protocol, because we have not summarised one. Every claim above is drawn from the studies below, and each of those links to the original paper.

What the pooled trials show

The direct evidence on whether EMDR works, and on what trauma therapy in general appears to be doing when it does work.

  • Does EMDR really work?

    Cureus · June 2026

    A meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials: EMDR clearly beats no treatment, but roughly ties other active therapies.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.7759/cureus.111244 ↗

  • Why does trauma therapy actually work? It may come down to changing beliefs

    Journal of Anxiety Disorders · January 2026

    A meta-analysis pooling 44 studies and over 5,000 participants found that the more a person's trauma-related beliefs shifted during therapy, the more their PTSD symptoms improved (r = .45).

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1016/j.janxdis.2025.103096 ↗

Whether people finish it

A therapy only helps the people who stay in it, and completion rates differ between the trauma therapies by more than their outcomes do.

  • Does seeing clients more often help them finish PTSD treatment?

    Journal of Traumatic Stress · July 2026

    A meta-regression across 84 study arms asked whether session frequency drives treatment completion.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1002/jts.70111 ↗

Trauma therapy in the harder cases

EMDR is one of several trauma-focused therapies. These trials test the wider family of them in the situations clinicians have been most cautious about.

  • Does trauma-focused therapy help when someone is also living with psychosis?

    The Lancet Psychiatry · July 2026

    305 people with psychosis and PTSD symptoms were randomly assigned to trauma-focused therapy plus usual care or usual care alone. Half of the therapy group saw their PTSD go into remission, compared to just 22% in usual care.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1016/S2215-0366(26)00090-8 ↗

  • Treating trauma and addiction at the same time in young people

    European Journal of Psychotraumatology · July 2026

    Clinicians have long debated treating them one at a time. This trial treated both at once, in ages 12–25.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1080/20008066.2026.2691364 ↗

  • Treating moral injury in veterans

    Psychotherapy · July 2026

    A pilot trial compared two therapies designed for the lasting pain of violating one's own deepest values.

    Read our summary →DOI 10.1037/pst0000632 ↗

Common questions

Does EMDR work for PTSD?

In pooled randomized trials, yes: it produced a significant reduction in PTSD symptom severity, and against passive controls such as waitlists it looked strongly effective. Against other active trauma treatments the advantage shrank and the two came out roughly comparable.

Is EMDR better than CBT or prolonged exposure?

The pooled evidence we have covered does not show that. Against active comparison treatments EMDR came out roughly level. Separately, a meta-regression across 84 study arms found people were more than twice as likely to complete EMDR as cognitive processing therapy, which is a question about finishing treatment rather than about how well it works.

Do the eye movements themselves do anything?

We have not summarised a study that isolates the eye movements from the rest of the protocol, so this page does not answer that. The trials pooled above test the whole package as delivered.

How good is the evidence base?

Mixed. Eleven randomized controlled trials published over two decades were poolable, and the authors flagged methodological weaknesses in most of them. That is enough to say EMDR beats doing nothing, and not enough to rank it against its alternatives.
